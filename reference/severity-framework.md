# Severity Framework

Two scales, stacked. The **flag** is what the reader acts on. The **severity** is why the
flag is what it is.

Apply both during Step 4 of the diagnostic process.

---

## The Flag — what this costs to resolve

This is the headline. It answers the only question a broker holding a requirements list
actually has: *do I spend more money on this deal, or not?*

| Flag | Means | The next move |
|------|-------|---------------|
| 🟡 **YELLOW** | Resolution path is known and cheap. The parties needed are identified and available. | Speed bump. Proceed. |
| 🟠 **ORANGE** | Resolution path is known, but the **cost is not determinable** without scoped work. | The next decision is how much to spend finding out. |
| 🔴 **RED** | Resolution is expensive, possibly prohibitive relative to the deal. | Price it in deliberately, or walk. |

### Orange is not a hedge

It is the most common real situation and the one a two-state scale destroys. "Might clear,
might not, and finding out costs something" is a precise description of a defect, not an
evasion. Say what has to be determined and roughly what determining it involves.

Forcing an orange defect into yellow or red is the single most expensive error this tool
can make.

### There are two roads to RED, and they are not the same

**Mechanism-red.** No instrument fixes it. A court has to decide, or an adverse or
unknown party has to be brought in. *(Severity: deal-killer.)*

**Economics-red.** Perfectly curable by ordinary instruments — and not worth it.

> Forty-seven heirs. All identified. All locatable. All willing. Running them costs more
> than the mineral interest is worth.

Signable. Curable. Completely dead. **A severity scale alone codes that `CURABLE` and
sends the broker straight into six months of signature-chasing.** The flag is what stops
that.

### RED is relative to the deal

A $40,000 cure is orange on a $2M tract and red on a $60,000 one. **Proportionality is
required.** If the deal value is not known, say which threshold you assumed and note that
the flag moves if the assumption is wrong.

## The Severity — by what mechanism it resolves

How to classify title defects as deal-killer, curable, or nuisance.

**These do not map one-to-one onto flags.** `NUISANCE` is always yellow and `DEAL-KILLER`
is orange or red — but **`CURABLE` spans all three**, and that is where most defects live.
Mary Elizabeth / Mary E. Smith is curable-yellow. Forty-seven heirs is curable-red. Same
severity code, opposite decisions. That gap is the reason the flag exists.

### DEAL-KILLER

A defect that cannot be resolved without litigation, quiet title action, judicial determination, or legislative fix. This defect alone can sink the transaction.

**Characteristics:**
- No curative instrument (affidavit, correction deed, release) can resolve it without the cooperation of adverse or unlocatable parties
- Resolution requires a court proceeding (quiet title, trespass to try title, judicial determination of heirship, declaratory judgment)
- The defect creates genuine uncertainty about WHO owns the property or WHAT was conveyed
- A reasonably prudent title company would refuse to insure over it without curative action

**Common deal-killers:**
- Intestate death with no heirship determination where heirs are unknown or numerous
- Ambiguous mineral/royalty reservation where executive rights are at stake and the amounts are material
- Competing chains of title from overlapping surveys or conflicting grants
- Federal tax lien within the statutory notice period with no subordination
- Adverse possession claim with color of title and insufficient evidence to resolve
- Wild deed in the chain that cannot be connected to any grantor in the chain of title

---

### CURABLE

A defect that can be resolved with a corrective instrument, affidavit, release, or stipulation without litigation. Delays the deal but does not kill it.

**Characteristics:**
- A known party can execute a curative instrument (correction deed, release of lien, affidavit of identity, ratification)
- The party is identifiable and locatable
- The fix does not require a court proceeding
- A title company would insure after the curative instrument is filed

**Common curable defects:**
- Unreleased deed of trust or mortgage where the lender is known and can issue a release
- Defective acknowledgment (missing venue, expired notary) correctable by re-acknowledgment or affidavit
- Name variance between instruments (misspelling, maiden vs. married name) correctable by affidavit of identity
- Missing spousal signature on non-homestead property correctable by ratification deed
- Entity authority gap correctable by filing a corporate resolution or certificate of authority
- Legal description with minor variance correctable by correction deed

**Time threshold:** If the curative action will take longer than the transaction timeline allows, a curable defect may function as a deal-killer in practice. Note this when relevant but classify based on the nature of the defect, not the timeline.

---

### NUISANCE

A technical defect unlikely to cause a practical problem. Most title companies will insure over it with or without a specific exception.

**Characteristics:**
- The defect is formal rather than substantive (a technicality that does not create genuine ownership uncertainty)
- A reasonable examiner would not consider it a risk to a purchaser or lender
- It has persisted for a significant period without any adverse claim
- A title company would insure without requiring curative action, possibly with a standard exception

**Common nuisance defects:**
- Minor acreage discrepancy between stated and computed acreage (less than 2% and no boundary dispute)
- Old utility easement that does not affect the intended use
- Recording in the wrong book series where the instrument is otherwise properly indexed and discoverable
- Stale deed of trust (beyond the applicable statute of limitations) that has not been enforced
- Variance between pre-resurvey and post-resurvey legal descriptions that refer to the same tract

## Edge Cases

Each of these moves the **flag**, which is why they matter more than they look.

**Curable defect with unlocatable party.** If the party who could execute the cure cannot be found, the defect escalates from curable to deal-killer and the flag goes red. **A cure nobody can sign is not a cure.** This is the single most common miscoding — the instrument exists, the law is clear, and it is still dead.

**Stale deal-killer.** Some defects that were fatal when fresh become nuisances with time — a deed of trust past limitations, a defect closed by a curative statute. Apply the limitations period *before* flagging. Age is the one factor that moves a flag toward yellow rather than away from it.

**Stacked curables.** A pile of yellows can total to an orange or a red. Six affidavits from six locatable parties is six times the cost of one, and at some point that is the answer. Classify each defect individually, then **say plainly that they stack, and show the count.** A broker reading six separate yellows will proceed; a broker reading "six yellows that total orange" will price it.

**Jurisdiction-dependent classification.** Some defects are deal-killers in one state and curable in another — Texas's affidavit of heirship procedure makes some heirship issues curable that would require a judicial proceeding elsewhere. Name the governing rule when the classification turns on it.

**Disputed vs. merely clouded.** A defect nobody is contesting and one with a live adverse claim of record cost very differently, even where the mechanism of cure is identical. Check whether anything of record shows the ownership is actually in dispute before flagging. An uncontested cloud on a 1958 deed is not the same problem as a lis pendens filed last year.

**Jurisdiction-dependent classification.** Some defects are deal-killers in one jurisdiction and curable or nuisance in another (e.g., Texas's affidavit of heirship procedure makes some heirship issues curable that would require judicial proceedings elsewhere). Note the jurisdiction-specific rule.
