# Title Defect Failure Modes

A taxonomy of title defects, each carrying its **typical flag** and — more usefully — **what
moves that flag.** Use during Step 3 (catalog) and Step 4 (apply the framework).

**Typical is not fixed.** Every flag below is a starting point that the five factors
(`rules.md` Step 5) move. The "what moves it" column is the part that does the work: it
names the fact you have to establish before the flag means anything.

---

## 1. Chain of Title Gaps

A break in the sequence of conveyances from sovereign to current owner.

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Grantor–grantee mismatch** | Grantor in a deed was never the grantee in a prior instrument. The chain skips a link. | 🟠 |
| **Missing conveyance** | A transfer occurred but no instrument documents it. | 🟠 |
| **Wild deed** | Recorded deed from a grantor who does not appear in the chain. Cannot be connected without additional evidence. | 🔴 |
| **Stranger to title** | Someone who never held record title executed a conveyance. | 🔴 |
| **Unrecorded conveyance** | Valid deed executed and delivered but never recorded. Affects notice and priority. | 🟠 |

**What moves it:** whether the missing party is *identifiable and alive*. A gap you can
close with a correction deed from a locatable grantor is yellow. The same gap where the
grantor is dead, dissolved, or unknown is red — you're into a quiet title action. Age helps:
limitations and curative statutes may have already closed a gap old enough.

---

## 2. Heirship and Probate Defects

Problems arising from the death of a title holder. **This category produces more reds than
any other**, and it is where feasibility diverges hardest from legality.

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Intestate death, no determination** | No affidavit or judicial determination of record. Heirs and shares unknown. | 🔴 |
| **Probate not filed or not completed** | Died with a will never probated, or proceedings opened and abandoned. | 🟠 |
| **Will not probated in county of land** | Probated elsewhere; authenticated copy never filed where the land sits. | 🟡 |
| **Heirship fragmentation** | An old intestate death compounded by subsequent deaths into a large and growing class. | 🔴 |
| **Unknown or missing heirs** | Heirs identified but one or more cannot be located. | 🔴 |
| **Defective affidavit of heirship** | Missing required elements or containing factual errors. | 🟡 |
| **Community property complications** | Surviving spouse's community interest not addressed in the chain. | 🟠 |

**What moves it — and this is the whole game:** *count and locatability.*

Three identified, locatable, cooperative heirs is a yellow — three affidavits. Forty-seven
across six states is red even though every one of them is willing, because the cost of
running them exceeds most interests. One unlocatable heir escalates the entire defect,
because a cure nobody can sign is not a cure.

**Ask before flagging:** how many, are they identified, can they be found, will they sign,
and is the interest worth the hours. A "curable" heirship defect with an indeterminate class
is a red wearing a yellow coat.

## 3. Instrument Defects

Problems with the documents themselves. **The most reliably yellow category** — most of these
are one signature or one notary away from cured.

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Missing signature** | A required party did not sign (e.g. both spouses on homestead). | 🟡 |
| **Defective acknowledgment** | Notary certificate missing, incomplete, expired, or wrong jurisdiction. | 🟡 |
| **Missing venue on acknowledgment** | Certificate does not state county and state. | 🟡 |
| **Unauthorized signer** | Signed for another without recorded authority. | 🟠 |
| **Entity authority gap** | Entity executed a deed with no resolution or formation evidence of record. | 🟡 |
| **Undelivered instrument** | Evidence a deed was signed but never legally delivered. | 🔴 |

**What moves it:** whether the person who has to re-execute still exists and can be reached.
A defective acknowledgment from 1994 where the grantor is alive is a phone call. The same
defect where the grantor died in 2003 and the entity dissolved is a different problem
entirely. **Delivery is the outlier** — it goes to whether title ever passed, which is a
court question, not a paperwork one.

---

## 4. Legal Description Defects

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Ambiguous description** | Does not uniquely identify the property. | 🟠 |
| **Conflicting descriptions** | Calls conflict with each other or with a referenced survey. | 🟠 |
| **Wrong survey reference** | Wrong survey name, abstract, or block number. | 🟡 |
| **Acreage discrepancy** | Stated acreage differs materially from computed. | 🟡 |
| **Missing exhibit** | Deed references an exhibit that was never recorded. | 🟠 |
| **Overlapping surveys** | Tract falls where two original surveys overlap. Competing claims. | 🔴 |

**What moves it:** whether a surveyor can resolve it or a court has to. A scrivener's error
in a survey name is a correction deed. A genuine conflict between senior and junior surveys
is a boundary dispute with an adverse party on the other side — and factor (c), *is ownership
actually in dispute*, decides which one you have.

---

## 5. Encumbrance Defects

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Unreleased deed of trust / mortgage** | Lien never released, regardless of whether the debt was satisfied. | 🟠 |
| **Outstanding judgment lien** | Abstract of judgment unreleased of record. | 🟠 |
| **Federal tax lien** | IRS notice filed against a party in the chain. Survives conveyance for the statutory period. | 🔴 |
| **Mechanic's or materialman's lien** | Unpaid construction work. Priority may relate back. | 🟠 |
| **Lis pendens** | Notice of pending litigation affecting title. | 🔴 |
| **Outstanding mineral lease** | Unexpired or unreleased lease conflicting with the transaction. | 🟠 |

**What moves it:** *does the lienholder still exist, and can they be made to release.*

This is the category where orange earns its place. An unreleased lien held by a bank still in
business is a routine release. The same lien where the institution failed and the note was
sold into an untraceable pool is a suit against an unlocatable lienholder. **Identical
defect, opposite cost, and nobody has determined which one it is** — that is orange, and
saying so is more useful than guessing.

**Age helps here more than anywhere.** Limitations may have already killed the lien. Check
before flagging.

---

## 6. Reservation and Exception Defects

Problems with language retaining interests upon conveyance. **The category where more
searching does not help.**

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Mineral vs. royalty ambiguity** | "Royalty" language that may have intended a mineral interest, or the reverse. Determines executive rights, bonus, and lease participation. | 🔴 |
| **Fraction-of-fraction ambiguity** | "1/2 of grantor's 1/2" — two-grant vs. restated fraction. | 🔴 |
| **Reservation vs. exception confusion** | Terms used interchangeably. Legally distinct concepts. | 🟠 |
| **Term interest expiration** | Reserved for a term or "so long as." Whether it expired is unclear. | 🟠 |
| **Subsequent conveyance of reserved interest** | The reserved interest was conveyed separately and that chain is incomplete. | 🟠 |
| **"Subject to" with unclear scope** | Carve-out does not identify what it carves out. | 🟠 |

**What moves it: factor (d) — does the law give a clear answer.** If the state's caselaw or
title standards settle the construction, this is priceable and often orange. If the language
sits in the zone courts have gone both ways on, no amount of record work resolves it. A
judicial construction or a recorded stipulation is the only route, and both require finding
and dealing with the other side.

**Flag these honestly.** The temptation is to call an ambiguity orange because it *feels*
resolvable. Ask whether more searching would settle it. If the answer is no, it is red, and
the basis is `instrument-decided` refuted by nothing.

---

## 7. Recording Defects

| Failure Mode | What it is | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| **Late recording** | Recorded long after execution; intervening BFPs may have superior rights. | 🟠 |
| **Wrong county** | Recorded where the land is not situated. Constructive notice may fail. | 🟡 |
| **Mis-indexed** | Recorded but indexed under the wrong name, tract, or book. Invisible to a search. | 🟡 |
| **Wrong book** | Recorded in the wrong series. | 🟡 |

**What moves it:** whether anyone acquired rights in the gap. A late-recorded deed with no
intervening conveyance is a technicality. The same deed with a bona fide purchaser in the
interval is a priority fight with an adverse party.

**Mis-indexing is a search-quality finding as much as a title defect.** If you found one
mis-indexed instrument, the search that missed it may have missed others — which weakens
every `record-searched` basis in the file. Say so.

## Reading the taxonomy

Three patterns worth carrying:

**Section 3 is mostly yellow. Section 2 is mostly red.** Instrument defects are paperwork;
heirship defects are people. Paperwork can be re-executed. People have to be found, and some
of them cannot be.

**Section 5 is where orange lives.** Encumbrances turn on whether a counterparty still
exists — a question with a cheap answer that nobody has looked up yet.

**Section 6 is where searching stops helping.** Ambiguous granting language is settled by a
court or a stipulation. More record work produces more confidence about the wrong question.
