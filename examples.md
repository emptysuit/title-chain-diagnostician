# Worked Calls

Three examples showing the full reasoning. Each demonstrates the flag, the evidentiary
basis, cause-vs-symptom separation, and where the call stops.

---

## Example 1: The Scattered Heirs

### Input

> We're acquiring the mineral interest under Section 12, Block H, H&TC Ry. Co. Survey.
> Roughly a 320-acre mineral tract; we'd be into it around $180,000 at our offer. Our
> landman identified the surface owner and a few mineral owners, but we can't get enough
> signatures to close. There are people all over the country who might own a piece. Six
> months in. **Do we keep paying him to work it, or is this dead?**

### The Call

**🔴 RED — deal-killer.** Judicial determination of heirship required, and the heir class is
indeterminate, so the cost cannot even be scoped until the determination is underway. This
is mechanism-red: no instrument reaches it.

**The Decision:** Stop paying the landman to chase signatures. He isn't failing — the task
is impossible as constructed, because the people he'd need to sign have never been
determined. On a $180,000 acquisition, a determination of heirship plus pursuit of an
unknown class is disproportionate. If you want this tract, the route is a determination
proceeding priced as its own project, not more signature work.

**Primary Cause:** The 1947 intestate death of Harold T. Mitchell, owner of an undivided
1/2 mineral interest in Section 12, was never probated. No will, no probate, no affidavit
of heirship, and no judicial determination of heirship appears of record. Mitchell's
interest has passed by intestate succession through three generations to an indeterminate
class of heirs whose identities and fractional shares cannot be established from the title
records.

**Basis:** `record-searched`. County deed, probate, and heirship records searched from the
1923 Mitchell–Barton deed forward. No probate of Harold T. Mitchell (d. 11/3/1947), no
affidavit of heirship, and no judicial determination appears of record. The 2003 mineral
deed from "Donna Mitchell, heir of Harold T. Mitchell" (Doc. No. 2003-04412) is of record
but establishes nothing about her status, authority, or share.

*Refuted by:* any probate, affidavit of heirship, or judicial determination for Harold T.
Mitchell. **Note the limit of this basis** — an absence is only as good as the search behind
it. If Mitchell died outside this county, a probate may sit in the records of his county of
residence and would not appear here. That possibility is not resolved.

**Reasoning Chain:** The chain begins with the sovereign patent to J.R. Collins (1901), who
conveyed to Mitchell and Barton as tenants in common by deed dated April 8, 1923 (Volume
42, Page 118). The Barton 1/2 mineral interest traces cleanly through three subsequent
conveyances to the current identified mineral owner. The Mitchell 1/2 stops cold at a death
certificate dated November 3, 1947. No instrument of record connects Mitchell's interest to
any successor. The next instrument purporting to convey Mitchell minerals is the 2003 deed
from Donna Mitchell covering an undivided 1/8 — but without a probate or heirship
determination, her status as heir, her authority to convey, and her fractional share are
all unestablished of record.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "Too many people might own a piece and we can't find them" is the
symptom. The cause is the unresolved 1947 heirship. Until Mitchell's heirs are determined,
nobody knows who owns what fraction. **The landman isn't dealing with a known group of
owners who are hard to reach. He's dealing with an unknown group who may or may not exist.**
That distinction is why more hours don't help.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Flag | Severity |
|--------|------|----------|
| Minor legal description variance between 1923 deed and current survey (corner reference corrected in 1956 resurvey) | YELLOW | NUISANCE |
| 2003 mineral deed lacks proper acknowledgment (notary certificate missing venue) | YELLOW | CURABLE |

## Example 2: The Ghost Lien

### Input

> Client is buying a 640-acre ranch, surface and minerals, cash, around $1.2M. Title
> company ran the commitment and everything looked fine until the buyer's attorney flagged
> an old deed of trust on Schedule B. Seller swears he paid that loan off twenty years ago.
> Buyer is threatening to walk. **Do we keep going or is this a real problem?**

### The Call

**🟠 ORANGE — curable. Resolves to 🟡 YELLOW on one check:** is First National Bank of Quanah
still in existence, and if not, is its successor by merger or FDIC receivership identifiable
and willing to execute a release? That's a phone call and a charter search. If the
institution failed and the note was sold into a pool that can't be traced, it escalates
toward red — a suit to quiet title against an unlocatable lienholder.

The defect is identical either way. **The cost isn't, and nobody has determined which case
this is.**

**The Decision:** Don't price this in and don't walk. Spend an afternoon on the successor
search — it's cheap relative to a $1.2M transaction and it settles which deal you're in.
The buyer's attorney is right to object; the objection just isn't costed yet.

**Primary Cause:** A Deed of Trust dated March 15, 1983, from Robert and Linda Dawson to
First National Bank of Quanah, recorded in Volume 287, Page 142, securing a note in the
original principal amount of $85,000, has never been released of record. No Release of
Lien, Release of Deed of Trust, or other instrument of satisfaction appears in the records.

**Basis:** `instrument-decided` as to the lien — the Deed of Trust was read on its face at
Volume 287, Page 142. `record-searched` as to the absence of release — Deed of Trust and
Official Public Records searched from March 15, 1983 to present; no release or instrument
of satisfaction of any kind appears.

*Refuted by:* a recorded release, or a satisfaction filed in the wrong county or misindexed
under a variant party name. **The seller's recollection of payoff is not refutation.**
Satisfaction of the debt and release of the lien are separate events; only the second one
appears of record, and only the second one clears title.

**Reasoning Chain:** The chain from patent forward is clean — sovereign grant, three
subsequent conveyances with matching grantors and grantees, consistent legal descriptions.
The current owner acquired by a 1996 General Warranty Deed from the Dawsons. The 1983 Deed
of Trust predates that conveyance and was not released before or after closing. The
commitment correctly reports it as a Schedule B exception. It appears as an apparent senior
lien encumbering the full 640 acres.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "The buyer is threatening to walk" is the symptom. The cause is the
unreleased 1983 deed of trust. The seller's representation may well be true, but the lien
of record survives until a release is filed, and no buyer or lender should be expected to
take title subject to it.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Flag | Severity |
|--------|------|----------|
| West Texas Utilities easement (1971) across north boundary | YELLOW | NUISANCE |

## Example 3: The Royalty-Mineral Ambiguity

### Input

> Trying to lease the minerals under a 320-acre tract. Bonus and three-year term would run
> us about $96,000. The surface owner says he owns all the minerals and shows us his deed.
> The attorney who ran the title opinion says "executive rights uncertain" and our division
> order analyst won't approve the lease form. Surface owner is furious. **Is there a way
> through this or do we drop the tract?**

### The Call

**🔴 RED — deal-killer.** Resolved only by judicial construction of the 1962 reservation or a
recorded stipulation of interest with the Bates heirs — who have no conveyance of record
and must first be identified and located. Mechanism-red, with an economics-red second layer:
even the stipulation route requires finding an unknown class of heirs and negotiating with
all of them before a lease can issue.

**The Decision:** Drop the tract, or restructure. On a $96,000 bonus neither route prices
out. If the acreage is strategically necessary, the realistic path is a lease from the
surface owner at a reduced bonus with the dispute expressly reserved, priced as though
executive rights may be half — but that is a deal-structuring question, not a title
question, and it is not this tool's call.

**Primary Cause:** A Warranty Deed dated June 14, 1962, from Eugene Bates to Carl Stoddard
(Volume 94, Page 311) contains the reservation: "Grantor hereby reserves unto himself, his
heirs and assigns, an undivided one-half (1/2) interest in and to all of the royalty in, on,
and under the above-described land." This language is ambiguous under Texas law. It could be
construed as (a) a reservation of a 1/2 nonparticipating royalty interest, leaving Stoddard
and his successors all executive leasing rights, or (b) a reservation of a 1/2 mineral
interest expressed in "royalty" terms under the estate misconception doctrine, in which case
the Bates heirs hold executive rights over half the minerals and must join any lease.

**Basis:** `instrument-decided`. The reservation language was read on the face of the deed
at Volume 94, Page 311. The record is complete on this point — the ambiguity is inherent in
the language, not a gap in the search.

*Refuted by:* **nothing further in the records.** This defect is not resolvable by deeper
research, and that distinction matters more than it looks. Searching for later Bates
instruments would establish whether the reserved interest was afterward conveyed; it would
not establish *what was reserved*. That question is settled only by judicial construction or
a recorded stipulation. Anyone who tells you more searching will fix this is wrong.

**Reasoning Chain:** The surface owner's chain traces from the 1962 Bates-to-Stoddard deed
forward through two conveyances (Stoddard to Whelton, 1978; Whelton to current owner, 2011).
None address, clarify, or modify the 1962 reservation. The Bates interest has no subsequent
conveyance of record. The examiner correctly flags executive rights as uncertain because
this "royalty" language is exactly the kind Texas courts have construed both ways — the
*French v. Chevron* line supports reading "royalty" literally; the estate misconception
doctrine recognized in *Luckel v. White* and its progeny supports reading it as "mineral."
Without a determination or stipulation, no prudent title company or division order analyst
will certify who holds executive rights.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "Executive rights uncertain" is the symptom — that's the attorney's
conclusion, not the cause. "The surface owner bought the minerals fair and square" is also
not the issue. The cause is the ambiguous reservation in the 1962 deed. **What Bates
reserved is the unanswered question**, and the surface owner's deed conveyed only what
Stoddard received from Bates.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Flag | Severity |
|--------|------|----------|
| 1978 deed (Stoddard to Whelton) misspells grantee in acknowledgment ("Welton" vs. "Whelton") | YELLOW | CURABLE |

## What these three demonstrate

- **Example 1** — the flag stops work that was never going to succeed. Six months of
  landman hours spent on a task that was impossible as constructed.
- **Example 2** — orange doing its actual job. Same defect, two very different costs, and
  nobody had determined which. The call is "spend an afternoon, not a retainer."
- **Example 3** — a defect no amount of searching will fix. `instrument-decided` and
  refuted by nothing, because the record is complete and the ambiguity is in the language.

In all three the tool stops at the flag and the reasoning. It does not draft the
determination, negotiate the stipulation, or tell anyone what to pay.
