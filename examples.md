# Example Diagnoses

Three example diagnoses showing the full reasoning process. Each demonstrates cause-vs-symptom separation, a clear reasoning chain, and classification of secondary defects.

---

## Example 1: The Scattered Heirs

### Input

> We're trying to acquire the mineral interest under Section 12, Block H, H&TC Ry. Co. Survey, Childress County, Texas. Our landman identified the surface owner and found a few mineral owners, but we can't get enough signatures to close. There are people all over the country who might own a piece of this. We've been working it for six months and we're about to walk away. What's killing this deal?

### Diagnosis

**Primary Cause:** The 1947 intestate death of Harold T. Mitchell, owner of an undivided 1/2 mineral interest in Section 12, was never probated. No will, no probate, no affidavit of heirship, and no judicial determination of heirship appears of record in Childress County. Mitchell's interest has passed by intestate succession through three generations to an indeterminate class of heirs whose identities and fractional shares cannot be established from the title records.

**Reasoning Chain:** The chain of title begins with the sovereign patent to J.R. Collins (1901), who conveyed to Mitchell and Barton as tenants in common by deed dated April 8, 1923 (Volume 42, Page 118, Deed Records, Childress County). The Barton 1/2 mineral interest traces cleanly through three subsequent conveyances to the current identified mineral owner. The Mitchell 1/2 mineral interest stops cold at a death certificate dated November 3, 1947. There is no instrument of record connecting Mitchell's interest to any successor. The next instrument purporting to convey Mitchell minerals is a 2003 mineral deed from "Donna Mitchell, heir of Harold T. Mitchell" covering only an undivided 1/8 interest (Document No. 2003-04412). But without a probate or heirship determination on file, Donna Mitchell's status as an heir, her authority to convey, and her fractional share are all unestablished of record.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "Too many people might own a piece and we can't find them" is the symptom. The cause is the unresolved 1947 heirship. Until Harold Mitchell's heirs are judicially determined, nobody knows who owns what fraction of his 1/2 mineral interest. The landman isn't dealing with a known group of owners who are hard to reach. He's dealing with an unknown group of owners who may or may not exist.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Severity |
|--------|----------|
| Minor legal description variance between 1923 deed and current survey (section corner reference corrected in 1956 resurvey) | NUISANCE |
| 2003 mineral deed from Donna Mitchell lacks proper acknowledgment (notary certificate missing venue) | CURABLE |

## Example 2: The Ghost Lien

### Input

> My client is buying a 640-acre ranch in Hardeman County, Texas. Surface and minerals. Cash buyer. The title company ran the commitment and everything looked fine until the buyer's attorney flagged an old deed of trust on Schedule B. Now the buyer is threatening to walk. The seller swears he paid that loan off twenty years ago. What's going on?

### Diagnosis

**Primary Cause:** A Deed of Trust dated March 15, 1983, from Robert and Linda Dawson to First National Bank of Quanah, Texas, recorded in Volume 287, Page 142, Deed of Trust Records, Hardeman County, securing a note in the original principal amount of $85,000, has never been released of record. No Release of Lien, Release of Deed of Trust, or other instrument of satisfaction appears in the Hardeman County records.

**Reasoning Chain:** The chain of title from patent forward is clean. Sovereign grant to original patentee, three subsequent conveyances with matching grantors and grantees, consistent legal descriptions across all instruments. The current owner (the seller) acquired by a 1996 General Warranty Deed from the Dawsons. The 1983 Deed of Trust predates that conveyance. When the seller took title in 1996, the deed of trust was already of record and was not released before or after closing. The title commitment correctly reports it as a Schedule B exception. It appears as an apparent senior lien encumbering the full 640 acres.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "The buyer is threatening to walk" is the symptom. The cause is the unreleased 1983 deed of trust. The seller's representation that the note was satisfied may well be true, but the lien of record survives until a release is filed. The buyer's attorney is correct to object: an unreleased deed of trust is an encumbrance on title regardless of the status of the underlying debt, and no buyer or lender should be expected to take title subject to it.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Severity |
|--------|----------|
| West Texas Utilities easement (1971) across north boundary of property | NUISANCE |

## Example 3: The Royalty-Mineral Ambiguity

### Input

> We're trying to lease the minerals under a 320-acre tract in Foard County, Texas. The surface owner says he owns all the minerals and shows us his deed. The attorney who ran the title opinion says "executive rights uncertain" and our division order analyst won't approve the lease form. The surface owner is furious, says he bought the minerals fair and square. Why can't we get this lease done?

### Diagnosis

**Primary Cause:** A Warranty Deed dated June 14, 1962, from Eugene Bates to Carl Stoddard (Volume 94, Page 311, Deed Records, Foard County) contains the reservation: "Grantor hereby reserves unto himself, his heirs and assigns, an undivided one-half (1/2) interest in and to all of the royalty in, on, and under the above-described land." This language is ambiguous under Texas law. It could be construed as (a) a reservation of a 1/2 nonparticipating royalty interest, in which case Stoddard and his successors hold all executive leasing rights, or (b) a reservation of a 1/2 mineral interest expressed in "royalty" terms under the estate misconception doctrine, in which case the Bates heirs hold executive rights over half the minerals and must co-sign any lease.

**Reasoning Chain:** The surface owner's chain traces from the 1962 Bates-to-Stoddard deed forward through two subsequent conveyances (Stoddard to Whelton, 1978; Whelton to the current surface owner, 2011). None of these instruments address, clarify, or modify the 1962 reservation. The Bates interest has no subsequent conveyance of record — no deeds from Bates or his heirs appear in the grantor index for Foard County addressing this tract. The title examiner's opinion correctly flags executive rights as uncertain because this type of "royalty" language is exactly the kind Texas courts have construed both ways. The French v. Chevron line of cases supports reading "royalty" literally (interpretation a); the estate misconception doctrine recognized in Luckel v. White and subsequent cases supports reading it as "mineral" (interpretation b). Without a judicial determination or a stipulation of interest between the current parties and the Bates heirs, no prudent title company or division order analyst will certify who holds executive rights.

**Cause vs. Symptom:** "Executive rights uncertain" is the symptom — that's the title attorney's conclusion, not the cause. "The surface owner bought the minerals fair and square" is also not the issue. The cause is the ambiguous reservation language in the 1962 deed. What Bates reserved is the unanswered question. The surface owner's deed conveyed only what Stoddard received from Bates — and what Stoddard received depends entirely on how the 1962 reservation is construed. The surface owner may own all executive rights or only half, and the title chain cannot resolve it.

**Secondary Defects:**

| Defect | Severity |
|--------|----------|
| 1978 deed (Stoddard to Whelton) misspells grantee name in acknowledgment ("Welton" vs. "Whelton") | CURABLE |
