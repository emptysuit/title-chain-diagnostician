## Primary Cause

A Warranty Deed dated 1961, Ostroff Sr. to Ostroff Jr., recorded at Volume 212, Page 88, reserves "an undivided one-half of the one-eighth royalty." This is a legacy double-fraction royalty reservation from the era when 1/8 was the near-universal lease royalty. Its quantum has never been fixed by any subsequent instrument, judgment, or stipulation of record.

Two constructions:

- **Fixed** — a 1/16 nonparticipating royalty (1/2 × 1/8) measured against gross production, regardless of what any lease provides.
- **Floating** — 1/2 of *whatever royalty* a lease provides, with the 1/8 read as a placeholder for the whole royalty estate rather than as a literal multiplier.

Your attorney's note frames these as equally likely. Under current Texas law they are not. *Hysaw v. Dawkins*, 483 S.W.3d 1 (Tex. 2016) construed a "one-third of one-eighth" royalty devise as a floating one-third. *Van Dyke v. Navigator Group* (Tex. 2023) went further and established a **rebuttable presumption** that a double fraction using 1/8 in an instrument of the estate-misconception era employs 1/8 as a term of art for the entire estate, not as an arithmetic factor. A 1961 instrument sits squarely inside that era.

**The presumption runs against you.** Absent something in the four corners of the deed that rebuts it, the reserved interest should be modeled as a floating 1/2 of royalty.

Executive rights are **not** at issue — both constructions leave the executive right with the mineral owner in your seller's chain. This defect does not block a closing. It halves a royalty stream.

## Reasoning Chain

The question is spend-versus-reprice, so the five open requirements sort on one axis: **does additional work change the answer?**

**Four of the five do.**
- Entity authority: a signature from a living, cooperative, locatable person.
- Name variance: an affidavit of identity.
- Federal tax lien: one document read, and a targeted index search.
- 1981 lease: a production search and a successor trace, two to three days.

**One does not.** No affidavit, no release, no correction deed, and no volume of additional record work fixes an ambiguity that lives in the granting language itself. That is the definition of the deciding defect under Step 6 — highest flag wins, and this is the only red in the file.

**The coupling that determines what it costs you.** The two constructions are economically *identical* under a 1/8 royalty lease: 1/2 × 1/8 = 1/16 either way. They diverge only when the tract is leased at a modern royalty:

| Lease royalty | Fixed (1/16 NPRI) — buyer nets | Floating (1/2 of royalty) — buyer nets |
|---|---|---|
| 1/8 | 1/16 | 1/16 |
| 3/16 | 1/8 | 3/32 |
| 1/4 | 3/16 | 1/8 |
| 1/5 | 3/20 | 1/10 |

So the reservation's cost to you is a function of whether Section 88 can be re-leased at a modern royalty — which is exactly what the 1981 Sandridge lease determines. **If that lease is held by production, the ambiguity is economically dormant for the life of the lease and you are buying a burdened, already-committed mineral interest. If it terminated, the ambiguity is live and worth roughly a third of your royalty position on any new lease.**

Determine the lease first. Then price the reservation at the floating construction, because that is where the presumption sits.

### On the 1981 lease — a scope correction

> "No production on our tract that anyone can find."

**That is the wrong search, and the absence of a Pugh clause is why.** A Pugh clause is what severs non-producing acreage from a lease held by production elsewhere. Without one, production anywhere on the described leased premises — all ~2,880 acres — perpetuates the lease as to **every** acre, including Section 88. Forty-five years of no wells on your 480 is consistent with a fully live lease.

What settles it:
- Texas Railroad Commission production records for **the entire leased premises as described in the 1981 lease**, not Section 88. Pull the lease and work from its description.
- Any recorded pooling designation, unit declaration, extension, ratification, or partial release. A partial release covering other acreage while retaining yours would be informative in the opposite direction.
- Successors to Sandridge Petroleum, if a release turns out to be obtainable. (Note: a 1981 "Sandridge Petroleum" is unlikely to be the modern SandRidge Energy, which traces to Riata. Do not assume the trace.)

If the lease terminated by its own terms, it terminated automatically — the cloud is the *record*, not the lease, and clears by recorded release from the lessee's successor, or by affidavit of non-production accepted by the title company, or by suit to remove cloud if no successor is locatable. Cost spread across those three is wide, which is what makes it orange rather than yellow.

### On the federal tax lien — a duration point your attorney's note did not reach

"Travels with the land" is correct as far as it goes. A federal tax lien under IRC § 6321 attaches to all property of the taxpayer at filing and survives conveyance. Ostroff held the minerals 1998–2013; the notice was filed in 2009; it attached, and it followed the minerals into the family LLC in 2013.

**But it travels only as long as it is effective.** Three provisions govern:

- **IRC § 6502(a)** — the IRS has 10 years from assessment to collect. After that the liability becomes unenforceable by lapse of time.
- **IRC § 6323(g)** — the notice must be refiled within the required refiling period (the one-year window ending 30 days after the § 6502 period runs) or it ceases to be effective.
- **IRC § 6325(a)** — and this is the operative one: **Form 668(Y)(c) is self-releasing.** The notice carries a date in column (e), and unless refiled by that date, the notice itself operates as a certificate of release the following day.

A 2009 filing implies an assessment around 2008–2009 and a column (e) date around 2018–2019. It is now 2026. **"No release of record" is exactly what a self-released lien looks like** — the release is printed on the face of the original notice, and nothing further gets filed. That is the trap in this requirement.

Two things settle it, and both are cheap:
1. **Read column (e) on Doc. 2009-04471.** You have the document number.
2. **Search the federal tax lien index from 2009 forward for a refiling** — and search it under **both** name forms. "Marvin J. Ostroff" and "M. Jay Ostroff" are the same defect as requirement #5, and a refiled notice indexed under the variant is precisely the kind of thing a single-name search misses.

If refiled, or if the collection period was tolled by a bankruptcy, offer in compromise, CDP hearing, installment agreement request, or extended absence from the country, this escalates. $118,000 plus seventeen years of interest and penalty against a $310,000 acquisition is economics-red on its own, and would displace the reservation as the decider. **That outcome is unlikely. It is not zero, and it costs an hour to rule out.**
